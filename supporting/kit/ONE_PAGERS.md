# SIX REPAIRS: ONE PAGE EACH

*One page per repair. What it does, what it does not do, who has already done it, the strongest objection and the answer to it, and where it can legally be adopted. Each section is self-contained and ends with the source line, so a single one can be copied out and handed to someone without the rest.*

**Every legal statement here is a starting point, not legal advice.** Where a repair is available locally, the model text is in the ordinance files alongside this one. Have counsel review before introducing anything.

---

## 1. YOU CAN TALK. YOU CANNOT PAY.

**What it does.** Prohibits transfers of value to officials from anyone seeking a decision from them. Registers people who are paid to influence local decisions, and publishes who pays them and what for. Stops officials from moving directly into paid advocacy before the body they just served.

**What it does not do.** It does not touch campaign contributions or campaign spending, and it does not restrict what anyone may say, write, publish, or organize. Volunteer labor is protected. Petitioning is protected. This reaches transfers of value and nothing else.

**Who has already done it.** Los Angeles voters banned lobbyist gifts to city officials by ballot measure in 2006, and the city requires registration once paid lobbying passes thirty hours in a quarter, with quarterly reports to its ethics commission. Chicago requires registration, annual training, and twice-yearly disclosure for any paid attempt to influence city officials. Denver, Seattle, Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Houston run their own municipal lobbying reporting. Honolulu's council adopted a gift ban covering solicitation and receipt from prohibited sources. The California Institute for Local Government publishes a list of roughly twenty California cities and counties with some form of lobbyist registration, and states on its face that the list is not exhaustive. This is ordinary municipal law, not a frontier.

**Strongest objection, and the answer.** *This is a solution in search of a problem in a town this size, and it will bury volunteers in paperwork.* Concede the paperwork risk, because it is real and it is how these ordinances die. Then answer with the thresholds: the model text does not reach an unpaid resident, a volunteer, or anyone under a compensation and hours floor set locally. The registry exists for people who are paid to be there. And the problem is not scandal, it is that nobody can currently answer the question of who is paid to work the council, which is a question a resident should be able to answer in one click.

**Legal status.** The local form is available now and durable. The full federal form, a flat prohibition on financial transfer to officials and parties, reaches the petition clause and functions as a campaign finance restriction whatever it is called, and under current doctrine it requires a constitutional amendment. Check three things first: whether your state occupies this field, whether a state gift limit preempts or permits a stricter local one, and whether your body may set penalties. In Texas, counties cannot regulate lobbying at all while cities and towns above five thousand population can, which is the kind of state-specific fact that stops a measure at the first hearing.

**Evidence.** Grade A. Pew Research Center, September 2023: eighty percent of adults say campaign donors have too much influence on Congress, seventy-three percent say the same of lobbyists, and eighty-five percent say campaign costs make it hard for good people to run, at nearly identical rates in both parties. Note the limit of that evidence honestly: it measures the diagnosis, not this specific proposal. Frame around influence and purchase rather than spending caps, because support for spending limits is where the parties diverge.

## 2. EVERYTHING EXPIRES

**What it does.** Every ordinance, rule, fee schedule, board, and tax exemption carries an expiration date. Renewal requires a public hearing, a report on whether the original problem still exists and what compliance costs, and findings on the record. Each item gets its own vote.

**What it does not do.** It does not repeal anything by itself, it does not reach anything required by state or federal law, and it does not touch building and fire codes, criminal provisions, contracts, bond covenants, or vested rights. Those are excluded in the model text.

**Who has already done it.** Colorado and Texas have operated statewide sunset review since the 1970s and both regimes still run. Be honest that the precedent is at state level: a local body adopting this is copying a state mechanism downward rather than following a crowd of peer cities. The deeper precedent is older. Expiring statutes were routine in colonial and early state legislatures and fell out of use as legislatures professionalized, so this is a restoration argument, not a new idea.

**Strongest objection, and the answer.** *This creates enormous staff work and risks losing something important by accident.* Both halves are true and the model text answers each. The staggered schedule spreads existing law across the full cycle so no single year is unmanageable. A single non-renewable continuation covers a review that ran late. A supermajority may designate a provision as continuing where lapse would create a safety or financial risk, with the list of those designations published annually, because that list is the honest measure of whether the ordinance is working or has been defeated by use.

**Legal status.** Available to a local body now for its own output. A body generally cannot bind a successor's power to legislate, and cannot sunset what state or federal law requires. Confirm the boundary with counsel for your state.

**Evidence.** Grade C, and say so. There is no national polling on sunset review, and anyone who claims otherwise should be asked for the source. Argue the record instead: early American practice, two state regimes running for fifty years, and the PATRIOT Act surveillance provisions that carried sunsets, lapsed in March 2020, and produced no catastrophe. That last one is useful precisely because civil libertarians on both the left and the right can point at it.

## 3. NOBODY STAYS FOREVER

**What it does.** Caps consecutive terms in local elected office, with a required break in service before returning. Optionally caps age, with the warning below.

**What it does not do.** It cannot touch Congress. It removes nobody from office on adoption. In the prospective version it counts no service before the effective date.

**Who has already done it.** As of late 2025, fifty-nine of the hundred most populous American cities limit the mayor's terms, and Ballotpedia records that Miami joined them in November 2025 with a lifetime two-term limit approved seventy-nine to twenty-one. Nine of the ten largest cities limit municipal elected officials, according to U.S. Term Limits, an advocacy organization, so treat that count as advocacy-sourced. Surveys put adoption across all municipalities between roughly nine and fifteen percent depending on how the sample is drawn, with large cities far more likely to have limits. Where limits exist, two terms is the most common and three is next. Structures vary: Philadelphia limits the mayor and not the council, Los Angeles and Phoenix allow the council one more term than the mayor.

**Strongest objection, and the answer.** *Turnover hands power to whoever is permanent, which locally means the manager, the clerk, the attorney, and the people paid to attend every meeting.* This objection is correct and should be conceded without hedging. The answer is that it argues for adopting this alongside the influence ordinance and the sunset ordinance rather than instead of them, because those two aim at exactly the permanent interests that gain from turnover. Three terms with a required break is also a more defensible settlement than two, since it preserves more institutional memory inside the body.

**Legal status.** For local office this is usually a charter amendment or ballot measure rather than an ordinary ordinance, because the term of an elected office is typically fixed by charter or state statute. For a school board, district, or union local it is the enabling statute or the bylaws. For Congress it requires a constitutional amendment: *U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton* (1995) held that states cannot add qualifications for congressional office. A local resolution urging Congress is symbolic, and should be introduced as symbolic or not at all.

**Evidence.** Grade A, and the strongest of the six. NPR, PBS News, and Marist, fielded in late April 2026: eighty-three percent for congressional term limits, seventy-eight percent of Democrats and eighty-nine percent of Republicans, with little variation by generation, and eighty percent for a maximum age. Pew found eighty-seven percent in 2023. The University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation has measured it at eighty-three percent with no movement since 2017. **One honest caveat to carry into a local meeting:** all of that polling asks about Congress. Local adoption at nine to fifteen percent nationally suggests that the demand does not automatically transfer, so do not tell a council that eighty-three percent of their residents want this. Tell them that eighty-three percent want it for Congress, that no legislature can deliver that, and that this is the part their own residents can vote on.

**On age caps.** They poll nearly as well and stand on weaker legal ground, which varies by state constitution. Run them as a separate measure so a challenge to the cap does not take the term limit with it.

## 4. PAY FOR CURES, NOT FOR MANAGEMENT

**What it does.** Public money offers a defined prize for curing a high-harm disease, paid on verified milestones with independent replication, and the result is manufactured at cost. It pays for the end of a condition rather than for its indefinite treatment.

**What it does not do.** It does not seize patents, ban private research, or set prices across the industry. It adds a public buyer for an outcome nobody currently pays for.

**Who has already done it.** Nobody, at this shape and scale, which is the honest answer. The adjacent precedents are real: prize-and-milestone procurement is ordinary federal practice, and advance market commitments have been used to pull vaccine supply into existence. This is the one repair with no direct precedent to point at.

**Strongest objection, and the answer.** *Government cannot pick which diseases to solve, and a prize large enough to matter is a windfall for whoever was closest anyway.* Concede that prize design is genuinely hard and that a badly designed bounty pays for luck. The answer is in the structure rather than the intent: milestone payment rather than lump sum, independent replication before award, clawback for fraud or partial delivery, and open manufacturing specification as a condition of payment. And the counterfactual is not a neutral market. The current structure pays more for managing a condition for thirty years than for ending it, and that is a choice already being made.

**Legal status.** Federal. There is no local version of this repair, and a local body should not be handed model text implying otherwise.

**Evidence.** Grade B on the sentiment, C on the mechanism. The polling showing large bipartisan anger at drug pricing was commissioned by organizations with a position, one of them using a bipartisan polling team, and that should be disclosed every time it is cited. Nobody has polled bounty-funded cure development at all. The honest framing: the problem is one of the few things Americans agree on, and this is one proposed answer, not a popular demand.

## 5. CREDIT REPORTS THAT REFLECT WHO A PERSON IS NOW

**What it does.** Ends the practice of a resolved financial event following a person for a fixed number of years regardless of their actual conduct since. Restores standing on the timeline of recovery.

**What it does not do.** It does not erase debts, forbid lenders from assessing risk, or hide current delinquency.

**Who has already done it, and the complication.** Colorado enacted the first state law in the country prohibiting consumer reporting agencies from including medical debt on credit reports, HB 23-1126, effective August 2023. Other states followed, including Maryland in 2025. **Then the ground moved.** In July 2025 a federal district court in the Eastern District of Texas vacated the federal medical debt reporting rule and stated, in language not necessary to its holding, that the Fair Credit Reporting Act preempts state laws prohibiting agencies from furnishing coded medical debt information. Analysts at Berkeley's Center for Consumer Law and Economic Justice characterize that preemption language as dictum with no precedential force, apparently written to invite challenges to the state laws. Practitioners have since flagged Maryland's statutes as carrying real legal risk. Anyone taking this repair to a state legislature needs to know that before they walk in, and needs counsel on where their state sits.

**Strongest objection, and the answer.** *Lenders need complete information, and hiding debt produces worse lending and higher rates for everyone.* The answer is not that accuracy does not matter, it is that the current file is not accurate. The Federal Trade Commission's congressionally mandated accuracy study found that twenty-one percent of consumers had verified errors in their reports, thirteen percent had errors that moved their score, and five percent had errors severe enough to be denied credit or to pay more for it. A system that gates housing, insurance, and often employment, and that is wrong at that rate, is not delivering the accuracy the objection is defending.

**Legal status.** State, and now contested. The Fair Credit Reporting Act preempts most state credit reporting law, the medical debt carve-out was the proven path, and that path is now under a preemption cloud from a district court's dictum. Treat it as available and litigable rather than as settled.

**Evidence.** Grade C on polling, A on the underlying defect. The only recent survey found was commissioned by a company selling lending technology, so do not lead with it. Lead with the FTC error rates.

## 6. SHOW PEOPLE HOW THEIR FEED IS BUILT

**What it does.** Requires a recommendation system operating at scale to disclose what it optimizes for, and gives each person the ability to inspect and change the shaping applied to them.

**What it does not do.** It does not decide what anyone may see, post, or read. It is not a misinformation rule, a moderation mandate, or a harmful-content standard. Keep it clear of those words.

**Who has already done it.** No American jurisdiction has a durable version in force. California's 2022 transparency law was the closest attempt, and in September 2024 the Ninth Circuit held that its content-category reporting requirements were likely unconstitutional as compelled speech, followed by a consent judgment in March 2025 permanently barring enforcement of those provisions. A challenge to New York's similar law has been before the Second Circuit. The record so far is of attempts, not of precedent.

**Strongest objection, and the answer.** *This is the government putting its hands on what I am allowed to read.* Concede the entire concern rather than arguing with it, because the objection is a reasonable reading of most laws in this area. Then state the boundary: nothing here removes a post, labels a source, or ranks anything. It requires that the machine tell you what it is optimizing for, and lets you turn that off. The user, not an agency, holds the control.

**Legal status.** Federal, and constitutionally contested. After the 2024 Supreme Court social media cases, algorithmic curation is treated as expressive activity by the platform, and the Ninth Circuit's decision narrowed the disclosure path further: compelled disclosure survives only where it is purely factual, and a requirement that a platform characterize how it handles contested categories has already been enjoined. That is the sharpest available proof of the framing rule, which is that this repair lives as disclosure and control and dies the moment it touches content.

**Evidence.** Grade B, and the most framing-sensitive of the six. Sixty-eight percent support making platform algorithms available for independent review, in polling commissioned by reform organizations. Sixty percent want more control over the posts they see and seventy-five percent do not trust platforms to moderate fairly, per Cato in 2023. Cutting the other way, the same Cato survey found fifty-eight percent think platforms are better suited than regulators to set content rules, and a late-2025 survey found fifty-six percent opposed to government regulation of social media. On misinformation regulation specifically, Cato found eighty percent of strong liberals in favor against forty-one percent of strong conservatives, the widest partisan split anywhere near these six. Stay clear of it.
