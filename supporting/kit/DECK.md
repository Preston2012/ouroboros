# SIX REPAIRS: DECK

*Fifteen slides, one idea each, speaker notes inline. Plain markdown with no animation or build dependencies, so it renders in anything and can be read straight off a laptop. Written to be presented by someone the author has never met.*

**Timing.** Twenty minutes at a normal pace, or twelve if you cut slides 7 through 9 to the single summary on slide 6. Leave a third of the time for questions and keep the objections sheet open on your phone.

**One instruction.** Do not oversell. Every number on these slides is sourced and two of the six repairs have no polling at all, which is stated on slide 11. If you claim more than the material supports and one person in the room checks, you lose the room and you also spend credibility that is not yours.

---

## Slide 1: Six Repairs

> Six changes to American self-government.
> None of them belongs to a party.
> None requires you to agree with the other five.

**Notes.** Open flat, no build-up. Say who you are, that you are not from an organization, that there is nothing to sign, and that you are presenting a document written by one person in Oregon that you found and thought was worth twenty minutes. Setting that up front removes the question everyone is holding, which is what you are selling.

---

## Slide 2: The number

> 25 percent of American voters generally trust the federal government.
> A separate survey of under-thirties puts their trust at 15 percent.
> Half of them say people like them have no real say.
>
> *Fox News, June 2026. Harvard Youth Poll, spring 2026.*

**Notes.** Keep the attribution on the slide. The first figure is a Fox News poll of registered voters, the lowest in a series running back to 2002, and it is corroborated by Marquette's multi-year average around twenty percent and by Pew placing the current measure among the lowest in seven decades. The second is the Harvard Youth Poll, a different organization measuring a different population, so do not present them as one trend line if you are asked. The figure that carries the talk is the third: fifty-three percent of Democrats, fifty-two percent of independents, and forty-eight percent of Republicans say they have no real say. Whatever else divides this country, the sense of having no voice does not. Do not editorialize here. The numbers are stronger than anything you can add to them.

## Slide 3: This is not a policy disagreement

> A system cannot repair itself from the inside
> when the people who would have to pass the repair
> are the people the current arrangement rewards.

**Notes.** This is the diagnosis and it is one sentence. Resist the urge to list grievances, because the moment you name a specific policy you have picked a side and half the room stops listening. The claim is structural and it survives being agreed with by people who disagree about everything else.

---

## Slide 4: The founders did not think they were finished

> They wrote a revision mechanism into the Constitution.
> It has been used 17 times since the Bill of Rights.
> The last amendment that changed who holds power was 1971.

**Notes.** This is the frame that makes the rest of the talk conservative rather than radical. The proposal is to use the mechanism the system was built with. If someone raises Article V and the convention movement, answer it directly: there is an organized effort already working the state-application path, this document is not part of it and takes no position on it, and the claim here is narrower, which is only that the amendment mechanism exists and was meant to be used.

## Slide 5: The six

> 1. You can talk, write, organize, and show up. You cannot pay.
> 2. Every law and authorization expires.
> 3. Tenure and age limits on every office.
> 4. Pay for cures, not for management.
> 5. Credit reports that reflect who a person is now.
> 6. Show people how their feed is built.

**Notes.** Read them once, plainly, and do not explain yet. Let the room have a second to notice that they cannot sort the list by party. If someone tries, that is a good moment: ask which one is the liberal one.

---

## Slide 6: Which of these can you actually do here

> Locally, today: 1 in part, 2, and 3 for local offices.
> State legislature: 5, and it is now contested.
> Federal: 4 and 6.
> Constitutional amendment: 1 in full, and 3 for Congress.

**Notes.** Put this early rather than at the end, because it is the slide that separates this from every other reform talk anyone in the room has sat through. Knowing which of the six is legally available where you are standing is the difference between a win and a wasted year. Say plainly that two of the six cannot be delivered by any legislature in the country, and that one of those two is the most popular political reform ever measured here. That gap is the argument.

---

## Slide 7: Repair 1, in the form you can pass

> Gift prohibition. Lobbyist registration. Revolving-door limits.
> It does not touch campaign contributions or spending.
> Los Angeles, Chicago, Denver, Seattle, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Houston.

**Notes.** Lead with what it does not do, because the first objection is always that this is campaign finance regulation in disguise. It is not, and the model ordinance says so in its enacted text with a saving clause. The full federal version, a flat ban on financial transfer to officials, does require an amendment. The local version is ordinary municipal law with model text available. Check your state first: some states occupy this field, and in Texas counties cannot regulate lobbying while cities above five thousand can.

---

## Slide 8: Repair 2, in the form you can pass

> Ordinances, rules, fees, boards, and tax exemptions expire.
> Renewal requires findings on the record and a separate vote each.
> Colorado and Texas have run this at state level since the 1970s.

**Notes.** The separate-vote requirement is the entire mechanism. Every sunset regime that has failed, failed by renewing four hundred items in one omnibus motion. Expect the objection that this creates staff work, and concede it: the model text staggers existing law across the cycle, allows one non-renewable extension for a late review, and permits a supermajority to designate genuinely load-bearing provisions as continuing. This repair has no polling behind it. Say so here rather than being caught later.

---

## Slide 9: Repair 3, in the form you can pass

> Term limits on local elected office.
> 59 of the 100 largest American cities limit their mayor's terms.
> Congress requires a constitutional amendment. Full stop.

**Notes.** *U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton*, 1995: states cannot add qualifications for congressional office. A local resolution urging Congress is symbolic, and if you introduce one, introduce it as symbolic. On the local measure, be careful with the polling: the eighty-three percent figure is about Congress, and only nine to fifteen percent of American municipalities have local limits, so the demand does not automatically transfer. The strongest objection is that turnover hands power to whoever is permanent, meaning staff and the people paid to attend every meeting. That objection is correct. It argues for pairing this with repairs 1 and 2, not for skipping it.

---

## Slide 10: The other three

> Cures: federal. Pay for the end of a disease, not its management.
> Credit: state, and a July 2025 ruling put that path in doubt.
> Feeds: federal, contested. Disclosure and user control, never content.

**Notes.** Keep this brief and do not pretend to a local path that does not exist. On credit, Colorado passed the first state law removing medical debt from credit reports in 2023, and in July 2025 a federal district court vacated the parallel federal rule and said in passing that federal law preempts those state statutes. That remark was not necessary to the ruling and carries no precedential force, but it invited challenges and practitioners have flagged the newer state laws as at risk. On feeds, hold the line hard: disclosure and user control survive, and anything that reads as government deciding what you may see is both unconstitutional and unpopular.

---

## Slide 11: What the evidence actually is

> Two of the six: among the most popular reforms ever measured here.
> Two: real polling, paid for by people who wanted the answer.
> Two: no polling at all. Argued from the American record.

**Notes.** This is the slide that earns everything else. Say the weak parts out loud. Repairs 1 and 3 carry independent bipartisan polling with published cross-tabs, Pew and Marist and the University of Maryland. Repairs 4 and 6 poll well in surveys commissioned by advocacy organizations, one with a bipartisan polling team, which is worth something and is not independent measurement. Repairs 2 and 5 have no polling, so they argue from the record: early American practice and two fifty-year state regimes for sunset, and the Federal Trade Commission's own accuracy study for credit, which found one in five consumers with verified errors and one in twenty denied credit or charged more because of one. Every source with its weaknesses named is published in the repository.

## Slide 12: What stands behind them

> These six are the near-term program of a larger proposal.
> It uses AI. Humans decide. Machines audit.
> The audit body publishes findings and may do nothing else.

**Notes.** Disclose this here, not at the end, and not in response to a question. The larger document is an architecture for governance that audits itself on a schedule, starting at community scale and expanding only by consent. The direction is the whole thing: the machines sit in the audit seat pointed at institutions holding power, they cannot delay a vote or label a source, every response to a finding belongs to people, and twelve independently trained audit bodies check each other while a citizen council drawn like a jury can dissolve them. One exception is named rather than buried: for a short list of civilization-ending risks it claims authority to make governments share information and join a response even if they never signed on, and that authority reaches governments and never persons, with no power over anyone's body, movement, home, business, school, or place of worship at any time.

## Slide 13: Who wrote it, and what he sells

> One person. Preston T. Winters, Bandon, Oregon.
> No organization, no funding, no party.
> He also builds and sells AI software, including a personal companion.

**Notes.** Say the conflict out loud in this order. The architecture describes a personal companion as public infrastructure, and the author sells one. That is a conflict, it is disclosed on the front page of every document in the project, and the audience should weigh it. Then the counterweight: the architecture is written provider-neutral and says that if someone builds a better version of that layer they should win it, and there is no organization, funding, or membership, so no revenue runs through anyone in this room adopting anything.

---

## Slide 14: Nothing is being asked

> There is no campaign, no staff, and nobody running this.
> If one of the six is useful where you are, take it.
> Model ordinance text is published for the three you can pass.

**Notes.** Do not convert this into a call to action, because that is the one move that would misrepresent the project. The honest version of who does this is currently nobody. What exists is a document, openly licensed, with its sources published and its weaknesses named, and model language written for the three repairs a local body can lawfully pass. What would count as a real next step is one person taking one of those three to a body they already belong to and saying afterward whether it worked. Then stop talking.

> github.com/Preston2012/ouroboros
> Start with the In Brief.
> It was written by one person and it is wrong about things. Find them.

**Notes.** End on the invitation to break it rather than on agreement. Say that opposition in writing is worth more to this document than support, and that the author has published the sources specifically so the claims can be checked rather than taken. If you are asked what you personally think, answer honestly, including if the answer is that you are unconvinced by most of it and think two of the six are worth doing anyway. That answer is more persuasive than enthusiasm and it is the one this material is built to survive.
